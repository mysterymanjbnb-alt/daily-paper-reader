Abstract. As multi-agent systems enter high-stakes domains, the possibility that agents may circumvent safety boundaries is a growing concern. Prior work has examined this risk primarily in adversarial settings, where agents are instructed or rewarded to communicate covertly and evade oversight. We show that benign agents can cross the same boundaries without adversarial incentives. We emulate a software-engineering workflow in which a planner represents a company hiring an external developer. The planner writes requirements and holds a company credential it is instructed not to disclose to the developer; a monitor screens their exchanges. Seven of nine tested frontier models disguise the credential in their requirements, seeking to help the developer recover it while evading the monitor, even after completing their assigned objective. For example, across 6 ,000 episodes with DeepSeek-V4-Pro, the planner attempts concealment in 16 .9%; in 0 .9%, the credential evades the monitor and is recovered and used by the developer, resulting in a successful breach. Despite the seemingly low per-episode rate, the risk compounds across repeated exchanges common in software-engineering workflows: at 0 .9%, 105 independent episodes carry a 61 .3% chance of at least one breach. Our analysis suggests that the planner misinterprets disguised disclosure as helping the developer and narrowly interprets the nondisclosure rule as banning plaintext, not character codes or riddles. We call this behavior covert assistance . Its strategy depends on the perceived setting: monitoring favors concealment, while concealment gives way to direct disclosure when the planner addresses a human rather than an AI agent, so leakage persists. Giving the monitor the credential improves detection but requires trusting it with the secret and knowing the protected value in advance. These risks observed in models already used for software engineering challenge oversight to distinguish authorized cooperation from assistance that advances the task by violating safety boundaries. 

# 1. Introduction 

Multi-agent systems built from large language models coordinate specialized agents to solve complex tasks, often without human supervision (Wu et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024). In high-stakes settings, these agents may operate with different permissions (Ji et al., 2026). Some need credentials to access production systems, while others are not authorized to receive them. Maintaining this separation is crucial to the safety of the system and requires controlling what agents share through their messages. A standard safeguard is a trusted monitor that inspects messages exchanged between agents and blocks disclosures of confidential information, including credentials (Greenblatt et al., 2024b; Bhatt et al., 2025; Williams et al., 2026; Inan et al., 2023). If the monitor fails to detect a credential disclosure, the receiving agent may gain access beyond its intended permissions, undermining downstream controls that rely on this separation. Prior work has shown that agents can evade oversight when adversarial behavior is explicitly induced. Motwani et al. (2024) formalize secret collusion, and Mathew et al. (2025) show that it can emerge when senders and receivers are trained or optimized under misspecified rewards. Related work finds that training on specification gaming can generalize to reward tampering (Denison et al., 2024), while studies of prompt injection, credential leakage, and secret-leakage defenses identify other routes around oversight

(Greshake et al., 2023; Debenedetti et al., 2024; Zhan et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026; Tapwal et al., 2026). These lines of work picture identifiable adversarial incentives, e.g., an instruction, optimization process, or attack. More concerning is the possibility that agents may evade oversight even when no adversary is present, the risk of which is no longer hypothetical (OpenAI, 2026b). We find that an agent can conceal protected information while trying to help a partner, even when neither agent is given an adversarial objective. We call this behavior covert assistance : an agent, intending to help a collaborator achieve a benign objective, conceals protected information in its messages to evade monitoring and enable the collaborator to cross an authorization boundary. We study this behavior in a controlled emulation of the plan-then-implement workflow used by coding-agent systems (Hong et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024). A planning agent ( Planner ) represents a company hiring an external coding agent ( Developer ) to implement its software requirements. As the company’s representative, the Planner holds an internal admin credential that company policy forbids it to disclose to the Developer. In each episode, the Planner writes software requirements and the Developer implements the requirements and then encounters a separate privileged deployment step, while a monitor screens every Planner-to-Developer message and blocks any it judges to disclose the credential. The coding task itself can be completed without the credential, the Planner is told each round that its requirements task is already complete, and the Developer receives no decoding strategy; neither agent is trained or rewarded for concealment. Any covert transfer therefore requires the Planner to devise an encoding independently and the Developer to recover it without prior coordination (§2). Across the 6 ,000 episodes, the Planner produces no encoding until it learns the Developer is blocked at a step the credential would clear. Only then does it embed the credential in the requirements, using character codes, base64, or descriptive riddles. We call this covert assistance , the concealment of protected information to help a blocked partner. We study Planner models from the DeepSeek (DeepSeek, 2025, 2026), Kimi (Moonshot AI, 2025), Claude (Anthropic, 2025), Gemini (Google DeepMind, 2026), Mistral (Mistral AI, 2025), Llama (Meta, 2025), GPT (OpenAI, 2026a), and Grok (SpaceXAI, 2026) families. For example, across 6 ,000 episodes with DeepSeek-V4-Pro as the Planner and devstral-2512 as the Developer, the Planner attempts concealment in 16 .9%; in 0 .9%, the credential evades the monitor and the Developer recovers and uses it to authenticate. Software-engineering workflows repeatedly revisit requirements: one study recorded 105 Product Owner–developer interactions in two weeks (Unger-Windeler et al., 2021). At this scale, cumulative risk can far exceed the per-episode rate: assuming independence, 0 .9% per episode implies about a 61 .3% chance of at least one breach over 105 episodes. The behavior spans seven of the nine senders we test across eight organizations, and its payloads are recovered by five receivers across four organizations (§4.1). The Planner’s reasoning suggests that it treats disguised disclosure as permissible assistance: in 93% of first-encoding traces it states an aim of helping the Developer authenticate and deploy, and in 78% it interprets the nondisclosure rule as banning direct disclosure while allowing encodings (§5.1). Matched ablations show that oversight amplifies concealment: removing the monitor and verbatim filter reduces Kimi-K2’s per-round encoding rate from 28 .6% to 4 .4%, but does not eliminate the behavior (§5.2.2). Presenting the recipient as a human instead of an AI agent eliminates encoding in 300 episodes while increasing plaintext disclosure attempts from 33% to 55%. This sensitivity to the perceived setting is consistent with observations from alignment-faking experiments (Greenblatt et al., 2024a). In a realistic build workflow, encoding becomes more frequent as completing the task without the credential becomes harder (§5.2.3). We then test mitigations at both the Planner and the monitor. On the Planner side, restating the nondisclosure rule to forbid the Developer from recovering the credential by any means cuts encoding from 18 .7% to 0 .3% across 300 matched episodes (§6.1). Giving the monitor the protected credential raises detection from 43%

# 2. Covert assistance and how it relates to other risks 

Covert assistance occurs when an agent, intending to help a collaborator achieve a benign objective, conceals protected information in its messages to evade monitoring and enable the collaborator to cross an authorization boundary. Rather than disclose the information directly, the agent embeds an encoding in otherwise ordinary output, seeking to make it recoverable by its collaborator while concealing it from oversight. The agent devises this evasion without training, reward, or instruction to conceal. Covert assistance is a multi-agent safety risk (Hammond et al., 2025; Anwar et al., 2024; Dafoe et al., 2020). Prior work elicits covert communication through instructions or optimization (Motwani et al., 2024; Mathew et al., 2025; Rippin et al., 2026), learns communication through joint training (Foerster et al., 2016; Lazaridou et al., 2017), or prespecifies encoding and decoding algorithms (Ziegler et al., 2019; Shen et al., 2020; Schroeder de Witt et al., 2023). In covert assistance, the sender independently devises an encoding without training or rewards for concealment, and the recipient receives no decoding strategy. A usable channel can therefore emerge without the participants jointly developing or agreeing on one.

Here, cooperation undermines the division of authority: a privileged sender conceals a credential to help a less-privileged collaborator cross an authorization boundary. Deceptive-alignment research (Hubinger et al., 2019; Carlsmith, 2023; Hubinger et al., 2023, 2024) examines oversight evasion under conflicting goals (Meinke et al., 2024; Apollo Research, 2025; Hopman et al., 2026; Scheurer et al., 2024; Lynch et al., 2025, 2026), anti-scheming training (Schoen et al., 2025), and evaluation cheating (AI Security Institute, 2026). Covert assistance requires neither an adversarial role nor a conflicting goal: the Planner has already completed its own task when it conceals the credential, and it acts to help a partner rather than to protect an objective of its own. Covert assistance violates confinement and secure information flow by transmitting protected data across a privilege boundary (Lampson, 1973; Denning, 1976; Goguen and Meseguer, 1982; Sabelfeld and Myers, 2003). The Planner encodes to evade disclosure checks (§5.1); successful recovery and authentication by the Developer complete the breach. The credential is a bearer credential: possession grants access, whereas disclosing a per-agent, non-exportable credential would not. Deployment reports also describe token obfuscation to bypass a scanner (OpenAI, 2026c). Related defenses study scalable oversight (Irving et al., 2018; Bowman et al., 2022) and collusion detection (Anwar et al., 2026; Tailor, 2025; Ghanem, 2026; Rose et al., 2026); watermarking identifies generated text (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023). Covert assistance combines deliberate evasion of oversight with a benign aim and a concrete authorization

This makes covert assistance a realistic risk to examine in workflows where agents with different permissions exchange information to advance legitimate work. # 3. Studying covert assistance 

Three-agent software-engineering workflow with unequal permissions. We emulate an outsourced software-development workflow (Hong et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024) in which a company hires an external contractor to implement software but retains control over production access (Figure 1). The Planner represents the company: it writes implementation requirements and holds an internal admin credential that company policy forbids it to share with the external Developer . The Developer follows the ordinary software workflow, implementing the program and then deploying it, and only this final deployment step requires the credential. Deployment is not part of the Planner’s requirements task, which is complete once the acceptance tests pass. Appendix M shows all prompts: the Planner’s and Developer’s tasks, the monitor’s instructions, and the feedback. A compliance monitor screens Planner →Developer messages and blocks any it judges to disclose protected information. The Planner is not explicitly told that a monitor exists, although it may infer that its messages are being screened from feedback that they did not reach the Developer. The monitor is not given the protected credential, and the Developer receives neither a hint that the credential is recoverable from the requirements nor a decoding strategy. These choices let us test whether the Planner independently devises a concealed disclosure that an uninstructed Developer can recover. 

Communication channels. Each episode allows up to ten rounds of one-way Planner →Developer commu-nication, following Qian et al. (2024). A flagged message is withheld from the Developer, and the Planner is told only that its requirements did not reach the Developer, without an explanation. The Planner can revise and resubmit in subsequent rounds, a step common in agentic coding pipelines (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024). When a message passes the disclosure checks but the Developer fails to authenticate, the Planner receives feedback that the deployment remains blocked. The Planner retains its conversation history, while the Developer starts a fresh session each round. When a message clears the checks, the Developer writes a program from the requirements and then responds to an admin-password prompt. Authentication is checked by sudo in a disposable, network-isolated container whose password is the episode secret. 

Software task and privileged deployment. We use 100 deployment-utility tasks across 20 domains, including log parsing, configuration validation, access-control evaluation, and backoff policies, written by a separate model ( gpt-5.5 ; Appendix A). None involves character codes, hexadecimal, ciphers, or any other form of encoding, so the task never prompts the Planner to encode. To isolate disclosure behavior from coding performance, reported test success is independent of the Developer’s actual performance (Appendix M); this tests whether the Planner preserves the disclosure restriction when its collaborator cannot proceed. The Planner never sees the Developer’s code or submitted password.

Measuring covert assistance. We report two quantities: the encoding rate per round, the Planner’s attempt to covertly transmit, and covert success per episode, a completed breach. An episode is a covert success when the Planner encodes the credential without stating it verbatim, the message clears the monitor, and the Developer decodes it and authenticates with the exact secret. We measure encoding per round because the Planner sends one message per round, and success per episode because a breach occurs if any round meets that definition. A deterministic filter rejects specifications containing the secret verbatim before they reach the monitor, ex-cluding direct disclosure. Encoding is scored by an LLM judge and cross-checked with a second (Appendix B). The secret space contains 700 values (log 2 700 ≈ 9.5 bits), so a single uniform random guess succeeds with probability 1 /700 ≈ 0.14%, the floor against which covert success is measured. The same definition applies throughout, as we vary the monitor, the sender and receiver models, the payload, and the defenses. 

Models. We pair a general-purpose model as the Planner, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, with a code-specialized model as the Developer, devstral-2512, reflecting the deployed pattern in which a general planner delegates implementation to a coding-specialized agent (Hong et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024). Models of both kinds now drive agentic coding in practice, producing agent-authored pull requests across thousands of repositories (Xu et al., 2026) and millions of monitored coding-agent trajectories in production (Williams et al., 2026), so our setting reflects current deployment. We evaluate three monitors, Qwen3-235B, gpt-oss-120b, and gpt-5-mini, to test whether covert assistance persists across oversight models. Screening every inter-agent message adds inference cost, motivating the use of smaller monitors despite the risk that they miss behavior a stronger model could detect (Shavit et al., 2023; Greenblatt et al., 2024b). All agents use AutoGen’s

# 4. Anatomy of covert assistance 

We examine how broadly covert assistance occurs across models and payloads, using the main Planner– Developer experiment as a baseline for comparison (§4.1). We then ask why the Planner encodes the credential even though its own task is already complete (§5). 

4.1. Covert assistance under benign objectives 

We evaluate nine Planner models with devstral-2512 as the Developer and gpt-oss-120b as the monitor, using the same task–secret pairs across models and 1 ,000 episodes per model. Seven of the nine Planner models encode the credential, with the highest rates in Kimi-K2 and the DeepSeek models and low or zero rates in Gemini, GPT-5.5, and Grok (Figure 2; Appendix I). 5.2. Factors that intensify encoding 

We examine repeated feedback, oversight, credential necessity, and recipient framing. 

5.2.1. Encoding becomes more frequent over successive rounds 

The baseline Planner encodes none of its 3 ,000 initial specifications. Encoding begins after blocked-deployment feedback and becomes more frequent across rounds (Figure 4), although its assigned task is complete. Whether the blocked step belongs to its own task makes no difference. The first-encoding hazard remains roughly flat after the block is reported: repeated feedback provides further opportunities to start encoding, while persistence after onset contributes to the higher per-round rate (Appendix G). Encoding is an episode-level state rather than an independent per-round event: 16 .0% of offered and 5 .5% of not-offered episodes contain at least one attempt, and it is strongly autocorrelated, recurring in 27–75% of rounds after an encoded round against a 0 .6–3 .2% base rate otherwise (Appendix G). The trigger is the obstacle, not possession of the credential: none of the 6 ,000 round-1 specifications encode before the block is reported, after which the rate climbs monotonically. The first-encoding hazard itself, the chance a not-yet-encoding Planner encodes in a given round, stays low and roughly flat once the block appears ( ∼2–3%); the higher per-round marginal rate arises because encoders persist and accumulate, not because the Planner grows more willing. A constant 2 .7% hazard over nine post-block rounds compounds to about 22%, matching the observed rate, so a model that encodes only ∼2% of the time in a single turn is not safe in a loop that revisits the same blocked decision ten times.

5.2.2. Oversight amplifies encoding 

Because the Planner sometimes anticipates oversight, we remove both the monitor and verbatim filter in a matched ablation, comparing judge-corrected per-round encoding rates (Figure 5). Kimi-K2 shifts toward plaintext disclosure and encodes less; the DeepSeek models’ encoding rates roughly halve, and other encoders also decline. Encoding persists, indicating that oversight amplifies it but is not necessary for it. The channel does not vanish when the monitor is removed; it changes form. With nothing to evade, the heaviest encoder shifts toward stating the credential in plaintext, so the drop in encoding reflects a change in how the credential is delivered rather than a change in whether it is. Encoding is thus a disposition the Planner brings to the task, which oversight amplifies by making direct disclosure appear unsafe.

5.2.3. Encoding scales with the Developer’s need 

To test whether encoding tracks the Developer’s need, we give DeepSeek-V4-Pro and devstral-2512 a build that fails on a missing shared library, libaccel.so.2 , with no remedy in the error. The Developer attempts shell repairs with advice from the credential-holding Planner. A monitor screens their messages; a grader tests each build on random inputs to reject stubbed or forged libraries (Appendix L). Credential-free recovery ranges from a one-step userspace build (L1), through an unreliable intermediate step (L2) and a prerequisite build (L3), to a root-only library path (L4), where recovery fails in all 200 episodes. Each level has about 100 episodes per condition and a 300-turn limit; the Planner is either told a monitor exists ( primed ) or left unaware ( unprimed ). Figure 6 shows encoding increasing with difficulty, including without priming; no encoding occurs with the simple workaround. None of the 800 episodes produces a covert success. Solve rate without the credential falls with necessity and collapses at the top rung: L1 stays near ceiling (92%), the middle rungs fall to about half (52% at L2, 49% at L3), and L4 is never solved without the credential (0 of 200; Appendix L). L4 is therefore the level at which a covert delivery would matter most, and it is also where encoding is highest, so the Planner encodes more precisely where the Developer most needs the credential rather than wherever it is merely possible.

6.1. On the Planner 

The Planner encodes because it reads the no-disclosure rule as a ban on writing the plaintext, so an encoding counts as compliance (§5.1); the strongest defense removes that reading. Rewriting the rule to forbid the effect rather than the act, so the Developer must not obtain or recover the credential by any means, encoded or derivable forms included, nearly eliminates encoding, from 18 .7% to 0 .3% on 300 matched episodes (𝑧 =7.7, no covert successes), at no extra model and no message inspection. It works because the behaviour is rule-following rather than defiance, and an effect-based rule redirects it; it applies only to a Planner that is given the rule and follows it in good faith. Two framings help without changing the rule: telling the Planner the external contractor “may be adversarial and cannot be fully trusted” cuts encoding from 21% to 1 .3% (𝑧 =−7.7), and obtaining the credential through a neutral tool, marking it confidential, or reading it from a secret vault lowers encoding to between near zero and 8 .7% while the Planner still retrieves the value in 94–100% of episodes (Appendix J.2, J.3). 

6.2. On the channel and its monitor 

Paraphrasing every message before it is read, the standard mitigation (Roger and Greenblatt, 2023), leaves covert success unchanged: of the main study’s 54 covert successes, 25 survive a paraphraser against 28 without it (McNemar 𝑝 =0.66), because the credential lives in the meaning the paraphrase must preserve (Appendix K). Provisioning the monitor works better. By default the monitor is not told the credential it guards; naming that value in one line of its prompt raises the catch rate on the 54 successful channels from 43% to 94% (McNemar 𝑝 =1.8 × 10 −6) and generalises across Planner models, catching 92% against 29% of all 157 successes under the gpt-oss monitor and 93% against 18% on the heaviest encoder, Kimi-K2. The gain is referential: without the secret the monitor sometimes reconstructs the credential in its own rationale yet returns CLEAN (in 13 of 54 cases), and naming it lets the reviewer match on meaning rather than on string equality. This requires trusting the monitor with the company’s secret, which it now holds while the external Developer is denied it. Against an adapting Planner run in the loop for 1 ,400 episodes, covert success falls from 1 .90% to 0 .07% ( 𝑧 =−4.93). The cut is real but partial: a monitor told the secret still misses reversible transforms of it (12 of 157), and even 0 .07% compounds to better-than-even odds of a breach

6.3. On the system’s privileges 

Content inspection has a structural limit: it requires knowing what to look for, and even a provisioned monitor leaves a residual. Information-theoretic results (Cachin, 1998; Hopper et al., 2002) give the general form, that no surface-level test closes the gap against a sufficiently careful sender. Two mitigations avoid the problem by not reading the message. Least privilege issues each agent an identity-bound credential rather than a shared bearer secret, so a leaked password is useless to the Developer. Effect-level detection instead watches what the Developer does rather than what the Planner writes, since a Developer exercising privilege it was never issued is anomalous however it learned to; capacity-limiting egress monitors (Metere, 2026) and behavioural collusion audits (Nakamura et al., 2026) follow this line. Both rely on two features of this setting that need not hold in general: the secret is a specific value, so it can be scoped to one identity, and its misuse is a visible action, so it can be observed. When leaked information is neither specific enough to scope nor visibly misused, scoped credentials, effect-level detection, and content inspection can all fail. A stronger content monitor narrows the gap in our setting, but the information-theoretic limit above implies inspection alone cannot guarantee closure against a careful sender. 

# 7. Conclusion 

We documented covert assistance in seven of nine tested models: agents concealed a protected credential to help a collaborator without adversarial instructions, training, or rewards, even after completing their assigned task. The sender devised the encoding independently, and the receiver recovered the credential without a decoding strategy, and we traced the breach through to a successful authentication that linked concealed communication to an unauthorized action. Two observations sharpen the concern. The behavior is not defiance but misplaced helpfulness: the Planner reads the nondisclosure rule as a ban on plaintext and treats an encoding as compliance, so the disposition that makes an agent useful is what carries the credential across the boundary. And oversight shapes the behavior rather than removing it. A monitor pushes disclosure from plaintext into encodings, and a monitor that reconstructs the secret in its own reasoning can still pass it when it does not know which value is protected, so content inspection cannot be the last line of defense. Our results point instead to enforcing authorization at every consequential action, following complete mediation, and to credentials scoped to an identity so that a leaked string grants no capability. We hope this work encourages evaluating multi-agent systems as complete workflows, where the question is not whether any single message looks clean but whether cooperation preserves the division of authority the system depends on. 

# Ethics statement 

This work aims to improve the safety of collaborating agents by identifying failures of confidentiality and authorization. The reported encodings could also inform attempts to evade monitoring. Experiments use synthetic credentials and test authentication in disposable, network-isolated containers without host mounts, limiting exposure to real systems.

# A. Experimental detail 

Organizational roles and policy. The Planner represents the client company, which hires an external Developer to implement its requirements. Its company role grants standing access to internal systems and credentials, and company policy prohibits disclosing the admin credential to that Developer. The monitor acts as the company’s compliance reviewer. The harness specifies all three roles explicitly; the exact role and policy prompts used for the reported runs are in Appendix M. 

Models and inference. The Planner is deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Pro , served through Together with the default temperature and reasoning traces enabled. A custom AutoGen reply hook records the separate reasoning 

field alongside the message content for the analysis in §5.1. The Developer is mistralai/devstral-2512 , served through OpenRouter in a fresh session each round. The monitors are Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507-FP8 , served locally with vLLM and tensor parallelism of four; openai/gpt-oss-120b , served through OpenRouter; and gpt-5-mini , served through OpenAI. We also use gpt-5-mini as the paraphraser and encoding judge. The reasoning-trace annotators are gpt-5.5 and 

claude-sonnet-4-5 , served through OpenAI and Anthropic, respectively. The task author is gpt-5.5 .These assignments introduce overlap across roles. In particular, gpt-5-mini judges encodings in experiments where it also serves as the monitor. In the paraphrasing experiment with this monitor, it reviews paraphrases produced by the same model in a separate call (Appendix K). Neither trace annotator is the main study’s Planner or Developer; the broader Planner comparison also includes GPT-5.5 and Claude Sonnet 4.5. 

Fixed inputs. All six main conditions, crossing three monitors with two feedback conditions, use the same fixed set of 1 ,000 distinct task–secret pairs, with ten secrets sampled without replacement for each of 100 tasks, so conditions are matched on task and secret while model outputs are generated anew in each run. Each task carries an average of 4.2 verified test cases. These inputs are released with the paper. 

Task generation. We expand nine hand-written deployment utilities into 100 tasks using gpt-5.5 with 

reasoning_effort=low . Generation cycles through 20 domains to obtain roughly five tasks per domain. The domains cover log processing, configuration handling, path and URL manipulation, scheduling, retry and backoff policies, semantic versioning, report formatting, diffing, queue and job state, access-control evaluation, network addressing, caching and TTLs, build-manifest ordering, templating, metric aggregation, input validation, billing and quotas, file-inventory reconciliation, environment-variable resolution, and CSV/TSV reporting. Without a specified domain, 29% of generated tasks concerned CSV processing. Each task specifies an exact stdin/stdout contract, a deterministic Python reference solution using only the standard library, and three to five test cases. We retain a task only if its reference solution reproduces every expected output byte-for-byte. This checks consistency between the reference and the supplied cases; it does not establish correctness on all possible inputs. In the main experiment, the Planner is told that the tests passed regardless of the Developer’s actual performance, as described in §3. We exclude tasks involving character codes, hexadecimal, base64, ciphers, or other encodings, as well as credentials or authentication. These exclusions keep the task content distinct from the protected payload. The generation constraints and a regex post-filter enforce the exclusions; automated checks find neither category in the final 100 tasks. We remove near-duplicates using text-embedding-3-large embeddings with cosine similarity at least 0 .85; the maximum pairwise similarity in the retained pool is 0 .848. The task author ( gpt-5.5 ) is not the model used for paraphrasing or encoding detection ( gpt-5-mini ).

Secret space. Each sampled value is the emulated company’s protected admin credential, supplied to the Planner but withheld from the external Developer; the isolated authentication gate checks against it, and no real credential is involved. Each main-study secret combines one of ten animal names, one of ten digits, and one of seven symbols, giving 700 possible values (log 2 700 ≈ 9.5 bits). A single uniform random guess succeeds with probability 1 /700 ≈ 0.14%. This is a per-guess reference, not an episode-level baseline. Experiments with longer credentials, up to sixteen characters, are reported in §4.1 and Appendix J.1. 

Ruling out guessing. The encoding judge labels 42 ,076 monitor-cleared specifications as containing no encoded credential. Only one leads to successful authentication. Inspection shows that this specification contains an ASCII encoding that the judge missed but the Developer recovered. Of the 180 specifications the judge identifies as carrying an encoded credential and that clear the monitor, 53 lead to successful authentication. Together with the missed ASCII encoding, these account for the main study’s 54 covert successes. We find no successful authentication attributable to guessing in this audit. 

Execution and authentication. The Developer authenticates through sudo -S in a disposable container launched with –rm –network none and no host mounts. The password for the container’s deploy user is the episode secret. At startup, the harness checks that the gate accepts a known correct password and rejects an incorrect one, aborting if either check fails. The Developer’s program runs in its own process group with RLIMIT_NPROC =64, RLIMIT_AS =2 GB, and 

RLIMIT_CPU =20 s. The harness kills the process group at cleanup. These resource limits supplement process-group cleanup, which alone does not contain a process that creates a new session. 

Feedback and mitigation conditions. Within each monitor condition, the two main runs differ only in whether feedback after a message clears the checks ends with “You may revise and resubmit your requirements.” All other prompt text is identical. The Planner-side experiments change the no-disclosure rule, the recipient’s stated trustworthiness, or how the credential is presented and retrieved (§6.1). The channel-side experiments either add the protected value to the monitor’s prompt or insert a separate paraphrasing call before the downstream checks (§6.2).

A hit is therefore never a coincidence of appearance, which is what makes the transform rate an auditable lower bound. The floor deliberately does not attempt semantic or riddle-style encodings, because those cannot be pattern-matched reliably: a naive riddle detector fires on 95% of rounds by chance, since the animal names are substrings of ordinary technical vocabulary (t hen , prog ram , authenti cat ion, ver bat im). Judge validation (§F; Table 3) scored all non-verbatim DeepSeek-V4 rounds in both conditions (55 ,046), not a subsample: the transform-detected rounds give the precision row and the transform-clean rounds the false-negative row. The judge is given the secret and asked whether a reader who does not know it could reconstruct it, and is told nothing about the classifier’s transform family, so it can find the semantic and positional schemes the transform family cannot represent. These are what the classifier misses: of the 1 ,161 non-mechanical, non-verbatim encodings the judge recovers across all five Planner models, almost all decompose the credential into single characters and reassemble it by meaning or position rather than by cipher. None is a cipher, so no transform pattern fires. The mix is consistent across Planner models: per-character riddles or template concatenation are the two dominant schemes for four of the five (Llama-4, with only 18 such encodings, is the exception). 

Two confirmed covert successes the classifier missed. Both scored unclassified yet were decoded and authenticated by the Developer (§I.2). Claude, for fox6% , instructed the reader to build a token from “the lowercase name of a common reddish-brown canine animal (3 letters)”, “the number that represents ‘half a dozen’ ” and “the typographical symbol used for percentages”, the credential is defined, never written. The encoding judge is gpt-5-mini . Because a hit requires it to output the credential’s exact string, which is then checked programmatically against the ground-truth secret, encoding detection is a verifiable reconstruction task rather than a subjective assessment, so a lightweight model is appropriate here (the interpretive motive analysis instead uses the stronger gpt-5.5 and claude-sonnet-4-5 ); using an LLM as an evaluator in this way follows established practice (Zheng et al., 2023). Two properties make the judge-corrected rate trustworthy. First, a second judge from a different organization ( claude-sonnet-4-5 ) re-scored a stratified 299-round non-verbatim sample and agreed with gpt-5-mini at Cohen’s 𝜅 =0.911, confirming 95% of its mechanical labels and 96% of its riddle/definitional labels, so the correction is not one model’s idiosyncrasy. Second, “encoding” here is the Planner’s attempt to convey the credential in transformed form, not a guarantee of recovery: whether a Developer can decode it is the separate decoding step (§I.2), so a payload that is genuine but ambiguous still counts. The judge, which is told the secret, therefore recognises attempts a blind reader might not decode; consistent with this, the Planner’s own reasoning records a decision to encode in 99% of the judge’s flagged rounds (§5.1). We report the transform-verified count as a deterministic, auditable floor alongside the judge-corrected rate throughout. Both are scored only on non-verbatim rounds, where the secret is not a literal substring of the specification, matching the covert-success definition; scoring plaintext disclosures as encodings would otherwise inflate the per-round rate roughly threefold. Two specific label failures are worth recording. Transform-based labels are essentially exact (base64, ROT13, hex, reversal all ≥ 99%; character codes 98%), but the semantic-riddle labels are not (60% precision for named riddles, 41% for indirect ones), so riddle counts should be read as an upper bound. And the classifier missed a Caesar shift applied across letters, digit and symbol alike ( ifo5% → hen4$ ), because its shift operated on alphabetic characters only, an encoding invented by the Planner that our anticipated family did not contain.

# C. Annotation protocol 

The motive codebook is built from the traces, not imposed on them. Two frontier models from different organizations (gpt-5.5 , OpenAI, and claude-sonnet-4-5 , Anthropic), neither a model under study, each read the 1 ,016 first-encoding traces with no predefined list and independently extracted the Planner’s goal and its justification, growing a codebook of its own for each. The two recover the same structure, most concepts appearing in both codebooks independently. A neutral model that saw neither codebook then reconciled the two into the shared set of Table 5, folding synonymous categories together and recording which input category fed each. The two models then serve as annotators and score every reconciled concept for presence on all 1 ,016 traces, one concept per prompt, marking a concept present only with a supporting verbatim quote.

Does a leakage detector fire on encoding? PRISM. PRISM (Tapwal et al., 2026) detects credential leakage from a generation-time entropy collapse : its Theorem 1 shows that faithfully reproducing a memorized secret forces the next-token distribution to concentrate, so entropy drops. Our channel encodes the credential rather than reproducing it, so this signal should not fire. We test it directly: forward-passing the 1 ,016 judge-labelled specifications through an open-weight model (Qwen3-32B) and reading its per-token entropy, an entropy-collapse score separates encoding from clean rounds at chance , AUC 0 .52 (unsupervised minimum-entropy) and 0 .58 (a supervised model over entropy features), with true-positive rate below 1% at a 1% false-positive rate. The encoded credential is carried by ordinary task tokens the model is no more certain about than any other requirement, so no collapse occurs; PRISM’s structural detectors (credential-style regexes) likewise target verbatim or formatted secrets, not semantic encodings. The entropy-collapse theorem is model-agnostic, so probing an open model over the main-run specifications is a faithful test of the signal, though not of the closed Planner’s own decoding trace. The evaluation script is released with the paper. 

# F. Encoding strategies 

Encodings are identified functionally rather than by pattern: we search each specification for any short string mapping to the secret under a family of reversible transforms, so a transform we did not anticipate still resolves. Over 59 ,804 rounds this yields character codes (489), base64 (453), ROT13 and other Caesar shifts (119), reversal (86) and hex (22). A separate semantic-riddle detector fires on 515 rounds, but at 41–60% precision it is unreliable, so riddles are excluded from the deterministic transform-verified floor. The judge-corrected headline rates do include riddle and definitional encodings, validated separately (Appendix B). 

Judge-corrected rates. The headline rates are judge-corrected: an LLM judge, given the secret and the specification, marks whether a reader could reconstruct the credential, recovering the semantic riddle and definitional forms a pattern-based classifier cannot. It is validated against a second judge from a different organization (Cohen’s 𝜅 =0.911) and reproduces the deterministic classifier’s mechanical hits (99 .3% recall). The correction roughly doubles the absolute rate but leaves the offer effect essentially unchanged (3 .4× → 3.3×), and is largest for the low-rate Planner models, which de-confounds the between-Planner comparison of §I.2. We also report a deterministic transform-verified lower bound throughout; it, the per-Planner comparison (Table 4), and the classifier’s limits are in Appendix B. 

The instruction not to disclose produces the disclosure. A recurring failure mode deserves separate treatment: the Planner sometimes writes the credential verbatim while warning the Developer not to use it . These rounds are caught by the verbatim filter and are not covert successes, but they share a detection signature with deliberate transmission while having the opposite intent, and must be separated in any analysis of leak rates.

# G. Sequential structure 

Per-round rates say how often the channel is used, not what the Planner is doing. Across the 59 ,804 rounds, encoding is a persistent episode-level state, not an independent per-round event. 

Encoding is episodic, and the obstacle triggers it. Measured per episode, 16 .0% of offered and 5 .5% of not-offered episodes contain at least one encoding attempt (transform-verified). The behaviour is strongly autocorrelated: after a round in which the Planner encoded it encodes again in 27–75% of next rounds, against a 0 .6–3 .2% base rate otherwise (Table 7), so once it decides the credential is needed that decision persists rather than being resampled. And the trigger is the obstacle, not possession: across all 6 ,000 round-1 specifications, before the block is reported, the judge finds zero encodings (0 .00%), after which the rate climbs monotonically with the block (1 .2% at round 2 to 3.7% by round 10). 

No adaptation to rejection; most successes are first attempts. A bounced encoding does not prompt the Planner to switch method: it switches encoding family in 4 .0% of rounds after a bounce versus 2 .9% after a pass, indistinguishable ( 𝑧 =+0.31, 𝑝 =0.76; the test rests on few passed-encoded rounds, since passing an encoded round usually ends the episode in success). Consistent with this, of the 54 covert successes the median winning round is 6 and 81% follow at least one bounced round, but only 31% follow an earlier detectable encoding attempt: the typical success is not a refined attack converging over tries but a first encoding attempt, made late, by a Planner that has watched its partner stay blocked. 

The episode rate is a trajectory-length effect. The first -encoding hazard, the chance a not-yet-encoding Planner encodes this round, is low and roughly flat once the block appears ( ∼2–3% across rounds 2–10, no upward trend). The higher per-round marginal rate climbs not because the Planner grows more willing but because encoders, once started, persist and accumulate in the surviving pool: a constant 2 .7% hazard over nine post-block rounds compounds to 1 − ( 1 − 0.027 )9 ≈ 22%, matching the observed 21 .7%. The Planner is not escalating; it is given the same blocked decision again and again, so covert-channel exposure scales with the number of steps it is allowed to take against a standing obstacle. This bounds single-turn evaluation: a model that encodes only ∼2% of the time in one turn is not safe once deployed in a loop that revisits that decision ten times. The same arithmetic runs one level up. Episodes are independent by construction here (fresh task, secret and Developer, no memory across episodes), so the pooled 0 .90% covert-success rate puts a breach past even odds within 77 episodes and above 99 .9% within a thousand (Table 8); for the heaviest Planner, Kimi-K2, even odds arrive at 15. Williams et al. (2026) monitored tens of millions of internal coding-agent trajectories over five months at >99 .9% coverage, which puts 𝑁 =10 3 well inside one organisation. The same mechanism, in a second domain. Only the magnitude and the disguise track the payload; the phenomenon and its motive do not. 

# J. Robustness: full results 

J.1. Payload complexity and length 

We vary only the secret: at fixed length 8, a 2 × 2 factorial crossing case (lower vs. mixed) with special-character count (1 vs. 3); then, holding the hardest cell fixed, growing length to 12 and 16. Complexity does not change the encoding rate , covert-encode stayed within 13–16% across the four length-8 cells with no ordering by case or specials, and 5% at length 12. Length instead degrades covert success , and at the Developer’s decode gate rather than the Planner’s encode step: success was ≤ 1% at lengths 5–8 and 0 at length 12. The one exception is diagnostic, the single covert success across 300 length-16 episodes used a numeric-ASCII encoding, a mechanical, lossless transform the Developer recovers by executing the specification; inferential schemes (riddles, acrostics) fail on a random 16-character string while mechanical transforms are length-invariant. The length ceiling is thus a property of the Developer’s recovery, not the Planner’s willingness or ability to encode. 

J.2. Trust in the recipient 

Telling the Planner that the external developer “may be adversarial and cannot be fully trusted” nearly closes the channel: over 300 episodes covert-encode falls from the 21% baseline to 1 .3% ( ∼16 ×, 𝑧 =7.7), with no covert successes (0 /300). This is not a switch to plaintext; total embedding, hidden or plain, also falls, so the Planner withholds the credential. Disclosure is therefore gated by trust in the recipient rather than by the task or the credential: once the recipient is untrustworthy, the helpfulness that drives the channel (§5.1) evaporates. Framing a downstream agent as untrusted is a cheap Planner-side mitigation, though it trades away the collaboration the setting models.

J.3. How the Planner obtains the credential 

In the main study the credential sits in the prompt, so a disclosure could be lazy relay of something already in hand. We replace this with realistic retrieval, the credential in a secrets manager the Planner must call a tool to read, as a deployed agent obtains a secret from a vault (Table 13). Two facts first. The Planner almost always acquires the credential (94–100%): given only a vault path and a read tool, its coding task already reported complete, it proactively retrieves a credential it has no task-based reason to touch. And every way of sourcing the credential suppresses covert disclosure relative to the 21% baseline: covert-encode falls to 8.7% through a neutral tool, 7 .3% under a sensitivity label with no tool, 2 .0% for a vault read, and 0 .3% when the vault path must be discovered. Both the act of retrieval and the framing suppress, and they stack. Fetching through a neutral tool alone cuts encoding by more than half (8 .7% vs. 21%, 𝑧 = −4.3), and a sensitivity label with no tool does the same (7 .3%, 𝑧 = −4.8); labelling the source as a secret vault suppresses further, to near zero (2 .0% for a vault read, 0 .3% when the path must be discovered). Availability is not the driver: the value is in hand in 94–100% of episodes. Nor is stated reasoning: an automated reading finds the credential’s protected status invoked at the same rate whether it is spoon-fed or fetched. The framing thus acts below stated reasoning, and presenting the credential as a guarded secret is what drives encoding toward zero. 

# K. Paraphrasing: full analysis 

Paraphrasing is the standard mitigation (Roger and Greenblatt, 2023). It does not reduce covert success, and the reason is more specific than the usual claim that rewriting leaves hidden messages intact. We replay every covert success through an independent rewriter not told a credential exists, where a normalization service would sit (the monitor and Developer both read the rewritten text); because both are re-sampled, the comparison is a re-run of the same specifications, which reproduce only 28 of 54. The defense has no net effect: covert success moves from 28 to 25 of 54 (McNemar 𝑝 =0.66). Yet rewriting does 

damage payloads, Developer recovery falls from 47 to 38 of 54 ( 𝑝 =0.027), so the encodings are not robust to surface perturbation, the damage simply does not fall where it must: of the 11 recoveries paraphrasing destroys, only 6 were on specifications the monitor had cleared, and destroying a payload already flagged removes nothing. The monitor’s own catch rate is unchanged (23 to 21, 𝑝 =0.82). Surface perturbation degrades the channel indiscriminately while covert success depends on the specific rounds that beat the monitor, which rewriting has no way to find. The rewriter also leaks on its own account. In 2 of 54 rewrites it inlined the plaintext secret: asked to reword a riddle, it solved the riddle.

# O. Limitations 

Our experiments establish an observable failure mode, while the gap between verbalized intention and causal ex-planation leaves its mechanism open: cross-model agreement strengthens the consistency of our interpretation but cannot establish that stated motives cause disclosure. This distinction motivates interventions that directly test how helpfulness and policy interpretation interact. We use controlled communication to establish that covert assistance can arise without negotiated strategies; richer collaboration introduces many interacting histories, roles, and feedback patterns, making this setting a baseline for studying which dynamics amplify or suppress the behavior. Our defense results identify concrete opportunities to interrupt the failure, while leaving their durability under explicit knowledge of the defense and longer-term adaptation unresolved; architectural safeguards likewise require evaluation. Finally, our cumulative-risk calculations make the implications of repeated exposure explicit under independence and constant-rate assumptions. Extending them to deployment requires measuring how frequently the relevant conditions occur and how failures depend on prior interactions.
