TACO: TERNARY ABSOLUTE-MAX COLUMN-WISE
ONE-SPARSE OPTIMIZER FOR LLM FINE-TUNING
Jichao Jiang1, Cristian McGee1, El Houcine Bergou2, HanQin Cai1 & Aritra Dutta1
1University of Central Florida, 2Mohammed VI Polytechnic University
ABSTRACT
Full-parameter fine-tuning of large language models (LLMs) incurs substantial
optimizer state memory overhead, limiting the model sizes that fit on modern
GPUs. Existing approaches either compress optimizer state, abandon first-order
gradients, or change the update geometry while retaining dense state. The re-
cently introduced Muon optimizer reduces optimizer memory through matrix-
valued updates. Still, its geometry differs from AdamW and can lead to perfor-
mance degradation when fine-tuning AdamW-pretrained models. To reduce opti-
mizer memory without sacrificing accuracy or computational efficiency in LLM
fine-tuning, we propose Ternary Absolute-max Column-wise One-sparse opti-
mizer, or TACO, which follows Muon’s operator-norm steepest-descent view but
takes the geometric route further. TACO computes the exact steepest-descent
direction under a dimension-normalized 1 →1 operator norm by selecting the
sign of the largest magnitude entry in each column of two-dimensional weight
matrices. This retains first-order gradients while making optimizer state mem-
ory nearly negligible. Our practical TACO optimizer maintains only a small
set of low precision gradient components per column, reducing persistent op-
timizer state by 174× relative to AdamW8bit (from 27.7 GB to 0.16 GB)
and peak training memory by 2.9× (from 80.6 GB to 27.5 GB) on OPT-13B,
while achieving comparable accuracy and runtime. TACO further enables full-
parameter fine-tuning of 30-32B-parameter models on a single 80 GB H100 GPU
across multiple model families and tasks. Our code is publicly available at:
github.com/Jichao2357/TACO optimizer.

INTRODUCTION
Full-parameter fine-tuning adapts all model weights and remains a strong baseline for transferring
large language models (LLMs) to downstream tasks (Houlsby et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2021); however,
it often suffers from memory overhead. For instance, a 13B-parameter model occupies roughly
26 GB of memory in BF16 before allocating any training state. During AdamW (Loshchilov &
Hutter, 2019) fine-tuning, the two FP32 moment buffers add another ∼104 GB of memory. At the
same time, gradients contribute ∼26 GB, yielding roughly 156 GB before accounting for activations,
temporary workspaces, or an optional FP32 master copy; see the Pareto frontier memory-accuracy
trade-off graph in Figure 1. Thus, at this scale, AdamW’s persistent optimizer states alone require
roughly 4× the BF16 parameter memory.
Several memory-efficient first-order optimizers reduce this overhead while retaining backpropa-
gation, including state-quantizers (AdamW8bit quantizes optimizer states (Dettmers et al., 2021);
FlashAdamW combines 8-bit optimizer states with a compact master-weight representation (Gon-
zalez Ortiz et al., 2026)), factorized second moment methods (Adafactor uses factorized second-
moment estimates (Shazeer & Stern, 2018)), and low-rank gradient methods (GaLore projects gra-
dients and optimizer states into a low-rank subspace (Zhao et al., 2024), and APOLLO-Mini uses
a rank-1 subspace to estimate adaptive scaling factors (Zhu et al., 2025)). In contrast, zeroth-order
(ZO) methods avoid backpropagation and approach inference-level memory by estimating updates
from forward evaluations (Bergou et al., 2020; Bibi et al., 2020; Gorbunov et al., 2020; Cai et al.,
2021; Malladi et al., 2023; Boucherouite et al., 2024). Recent variants incorporate low-rank sub-

The recently introduced Muon optimizer (Jordan, 2024; Bernstein & Newhouse, 2024; Shah et al.,
2025) asks a different question: what steepest-descent direction under a matrix geometry makes
the update useful? Muon treats two-dimensional weights as matrices, retains a single momentum
matrix, and orthogonalizes it to approximate a steepest-descent step under spectral geometry. This
reduces optimizer memory relative to AdamW, although Muon’s state remains dense. Additionally,
switching an AdamW-pretrained model to Muon during fine-tuning can degrade performance, a
phenomenon called optimizer mismatch that is attributed to the different update geometries and
implicit biases of the two optimizers (Qu et al., 2026).
Zeroth-Order Muon (ZO-Muon) (Lang et al., 2026) improves query efficiency, but as a zeroth-order
method, it requires additional forward evaluations. Sequential evaluation for ZO methods can keep
memory close to inference, but the additional function evaluations can increase computational and
communication overhead, especially in distributed training or with larger batches.
Thus, existing approaches, including Muon, expose a fundamental trade-off between the memory
footprint of first-order training and the optimization efficiency of standard gradients, prompting a
natural question:
Can full-parameter fine-tuning retain first-order information and competitive wall-clock effi-
ciency while making persistent optimizer-state memory nearly negligible?
To answer this, we propose TACO, a Ternary Absolute-max Column-wise One-sparse optimizer.
Using the same operator-norm steepest-descent viewpoint as Muon, TACO is not just another
memory-efficient optimizer; TACO’s conceptual centerpiece is an optimization geometry whose ex-
act steepest-descent direction has extreme structural sparsity. Under a dimension-normalized 1→1
operator norm, the corresponding dual geometry makes the steepest-descent direction separable
across columns, and each column selects the sign of its largest-magnitude gradient coordinate. Thus,
an m × n update contains at most n nonzeros.
Although the TACO steepest-descent direction is one-sparse per column, minibatch gradients can
make the selected coordinates unstable across training. A conventional dense gradient history can
stabilize coordinate selection but erodes the memory advantage. TACO instead retains only a small,
low-precision set of dynamic gradient heavy hitters per column. This sparse gradient history keeps
persistent optimizer-state memory negligible even as model size grows. For example, TACO can
fine-tune OPT-30B on a single 80 GB H100 GPU, with peak GPU memory of 62.5 GB on SST-2,
64.7 GB on RTE, and 68.1 GB on BoolQ; see Peak GPU memory decomposition in Figure 1. Adam
does not fit this model on a single H100 80 GB GPU. Across our study, TACO enables full-parameter

fine-tuning of models up to 32B parameters on a single 80 GB H100 and transfers across multiple
model families and tasks.
Taken together, our main contributions are:
(i) Geometry-induced sparse optimization. We propose TACO, a new first-order optimizer for
memory-efficient full-parameter LLM fine-tuning. TACO is derived from the exact steepest-descent
solution under a dimension-normalized 1 →1 operator norm, whose geometry naturally yields a
ternary, column-wise Top-1 update. Thus, TACO’s sparsity is intrinsic to its optimization geometry
rather than imposed by post-hoc gradient sparsification; see Section 3.
(ii) Convergence and fine-tuning theory. We prove that vanilla TACO reaches an ε-stationary
point in O(ε−2) iterations under standard smoothness assumptions. We further show that, in our
theoretical setting, TACO shares the continuation geometry and limiting solution of an Adam proxy
while differing from Muon, and satisfies standard µP spectral scaling under balanced winner counts.
These results connect TACO to optimizer mismatch and the learning rate transfer problem; see
Sections 3 and 4.
(iii) TACO-specific memory-efficient design. TACO does not require an accurate dense model
of the gradient history, but only enough information to recover the dominant coordinate in each
column. Exploiting this structure, we develop a practical implementation based on dynamic FP8
sparse gradient histories and immediate gradient processing, reducing persistent state from O(mn)
to O(n) while retaining first-order gradients and the column-wise Top-1 update; see Section 5.
(iv) Empirical validation at scale. We evaluate TACO across model scales, architectures, and
downstream tasks. TACO achieves competitive task performance with substantially reduced training
memory, enables full-parameter fine-tuning of OPT-30B and Qwen3-32B on a single 80 GB H100
GPU, and transfers across OPT, Qwen3, Pythia, Llama, and Mistral model families. Ablations
further characterize the roles of the gradient history EMA decay factor, heavy hitter threshold, update
sparsity, and optimizer step budget; see Section 6.

RELATED WORK
Zeroth-order optimization has become a practical way to reduce the memory cost of LLM fine-
tuning. These methods construct an update direction from the change in loss under a parameter
perturbation. MeZO applies this idea by regenerating its perturbations in place using a seeding
trick (Malladi et al., 2023). Sparse MeZO reduces the number of perturbed parameters, preserv-
ing sparsity (Liu et al., 2025). ZO-Muon projects the estimated gradient into a lower-dimensional
subspace and applies polar orthogonalization to recover more useful structure from the noisy esti-
mate (Lang et al., 2026). HiZOO instead incorporates diagonal Hessian information into zeroth-
order optimization, using curvature-aware preconditioning to improve convergence (Zhao et al.,
2025).
Memory-efficient first-order optimization methods retain backpropagation while reducing
parameter-associated training state. Adafactor represents second moments using row and column
factors (Shazeer & Stern, 2018), while CAME adds a confidence-guided correction to improve the
stability of this representation (Luo et al., 2023). Alternatively, 8-bit Adam uses block-wise quanti-
zation to store optimizer statistics at lower precision (Dettmers et al., 2021). FlashOptim combines
quantized optimizer states with a compact master-weight representation and provides FlashAdamW
as a drop-in AdamW variant (Gonzalez Ortiz et al., 2026). GaLore projects gradients and optimizer
states into a lower-dimensional space (Zhao et al., 2024), whereas APOLLO approximates adaptive
gradient scaling through a random low-rank auxiliary space; its APOLLO-Mini variant reduces this
space to rank one and applies tensor-wise scaling (Zhu et al., 2025).
Operator-norm and linear-minimization-oracle (LMO) optimization. Bernstein & Newhouse
(2024) formulate matrix optimization as steepest-descent under operator norms and characterize the
1 →p family through maximum column norms. Pethick et al. (2025) develop a stochastic LMO
framework and instantiate it with layer-aware operator geometries in Scion.
Sign-based methods. In gradient compression, TernGrad maps gradient values to {−1, 0, +1} to
reduce the communication bottleneck in distributed training (Wen et al., 2017), while signSGD
retains only the coordinate-wise gradient signs (Bernstein et al., 2018); see GRACE (Xu et al.,
3


2021), a compressed communication framework that places these methods under one umbrella.
SignMuon (Mishra et al., 2026) communicates the signs of locally constructed Muon directions.

CONTINUATION GEOMETRY, IMPLICIT BIAS, AND µP-SCALING OF TACO
Recent work shows that Adam and Muon can exhibit different fine-tuning behavior because their
update geometries induce different implicit biases (Qu et al., 2026). We study where TACO lies
relative to these geometries in a simplified fixed-input setting, and then analyze how the spectral
norm of its update scales with matrix dimensions.
Let z ∈Rn, z̸ = 0, denote a fixed input. Ties in TACO’s column-wise selections are resolved using
a fixed deterministic rule. For W0 ∈Rm×n and a vector w ∈Rn, let the continuation set generated
by w at W0 be represented by C(W0, w) := {W0 + aw⊤: a ∈Rm}.

parameter space while producing the same target output.

PRACTICAL DESIGN OF TACO
Although vanilla TACO updates are sparse by design, applying S directly to a single minibatch
gradient makes the selected coordinates sensitive to minibatch noise. A conventional dense gradient
history can stabilize coordinate selection but also reintroduce the dense mn-entry state TACO was
meant to avoid. Exploiting TACO’s special geometric update structure, our implementation resolves
this tension with a sparse gradient history, low-precision state, and immediate gradient processing.
This motivates the following design principle:
TACO does not need an accurate model of the gradient history. TACO only needs enough infor-
mation to recover its dominant coordinate in each column.

in-place using the sparse gradient history, extracts Hk(Gt) as the new history, applies the parameter
update, and releases Gt. We set gradient accumulation to 1, so dense gradients do not persist across
microbatches. Gradient checkpointing reduces activation memory. Together, these techniques bring
peak training memory close to inference memory. Peak training memory includes model parameters,
sparse optimizer state, checkpointed activations, and the current gradient. Autograd hooks and
checkpointing are standard systems techniques rather than contributions of this work.

CONCLUSION
We introduced the Ternary Absolute-max Column-wise One-sparse (TACO) optimizer for memory-
efficient full-parameter LLM fine-tuning. TACO is derived from the exact minimizer of a local op-
timization problem under a dimension-normalized 1→1 operator norm, yielding a ternary, column-
wise one-sparse update. Exploiting this structure, practical TACO maintains a sparse, low-precision
9


gradient history for matrix-valued parameters, making persistent optimizer-state memory nearly
negligible. We establish a nonconvex convergence guarantee for vanilla TACO and, in a simplified
fixed-input setting, show that it shares the continuation geometry and limiting solution of an Adam
proxy while generally differing from Muon. We further establish µP spectral scaling under balanced
winner counts and give a sufficient condition under which sparse history preserves the dense-history
TACO update. Across model scales, architectures, and downstream tasks, TACO maintains compet-
itive performance with a small memory footprint, enabling full-parameter fine-tuning of models up
to 32B parameters on a single 80 GB H100 GPU. More broadly, our results suggest that optimization
geometry can provide a useful framework for designing memory-efficient first-order optimizers.

Implementation details. TACO treats model parameters as matrices and is applied to matrix-valued
parameters. For ordinary attention and FFN linear weight matrices, TACO uses the geometry-
derived m/n scaling in (5). Token embedding matrices are treated separately and use a scaling
factor of 1, reflecting their one-hot lookup structure rather than an ordinary dense linear transfor-
mation. Following the common practice of matrix-based optimizers such as Muon (Jordan, 2024),
scalar and vector parameters are optimized with an auxiliary AdamW optimizer. For models based
on the GPT-NeoX architecture, such as Pythia, the query, key, and value projections are stored in
a single fused QKV parameter. We respect this logical matrix structure by treating the fused pa-
rameter as three separate Q, K, and V projections for TACO history compression and coordinate
selection, and applying TACO independently to each projection before writing the updates back to
the fused parameter. This prevents the Q, K, and V projections from competing for a single selected
coordinate in each column.
