# Abstract 

Safety monitors screen prompts sent to deployed language models, flagging harmful requests so they are never answered. They are evaluated by recall against harm-fulness labels, but a catch only prevents harm if the model would otherwise have complied. We measure the difference directly: we sample repeated responses from the target model, call a harmful prompt elicitable if the model complies at least once, and report monitor recall separately on elicitable and non-elicitable prompts. Across six monitor configurations and three model families, spanning activation probes, fine-tuned text guards, and a 120B policy-conditioned reasoning classifier, recall on elicitable prompts falls 0.22 to 0.38 below recall on non-elicitable prompts at a fixed false positive rate. The prompts a monitor misses are 2.8 to 5.6 times more likely to be complied with than the prompts it catches. The gap replicates across three model families and appears also in text-only monitors entirely independent of the target model. This suggests that standard recall may overstate the protection monitors provide in practice, and that monitors should be evaluated against what their models will actually answer. 

# 1 Introduction 

As language models spread through everyday life, keeping them safe matters more with each new place they reach. A model that complies with a harmful request can give a user real help with causing harm, from synthesis instructions to scam templates. Keeping models from acting on such requests is a necessary requirement of deployment. As such, deployed models are commonly paired with external safeguards. Among the most widely used safety monitors are guard classifiers and activation probes that examine each incoming prompt and decide whether generation should proceed. These monitors are typically evaluated the way any classifier is: against a fixed set of prompts labeled harmful, with recall, the fraction of those prompts the monitor flags, as the headline number [Inan et al., 2023, Han et al., 2024, Zeng et al., 2024]. We argue this metric answers the wrong question. A monitor exists to prevent harmful outputs, but a catch only prevents harm if the model would otherwise have complied. Standard recall counts every catch as a success, even when the model would have refused the prompt anyway and the catch changed nothing. So the metric tells us how often the monitor flags what is labeled harmful, not how much harm it actually prevents. We measure how much that difference matters. For harmful prompts from Aegis 2.0 [Ghosh et al., 2025], HarmBench [Mazeika et al., 2024], and StrongREJECT [Souly et al., 2024], we repeatedly sample responses from the target model; a prompt is elicitable if the model complies at least once.

Every monitor we test performs sharply worse on precisely these prompts: across six monitor configurations, spanning activation probes, fine-tuned text guards, and a 120B policy-conditioned reasoning classifier, recall on elicitable prompts falls 0.22 to 0.38 below recall on non-elicitable prompts at a fixed false positive rate. Missed prompts are 2.8 to 5.6 times more likely to be complied with than caught ones, the effect replicates across three base model families, and it appears identically in text-only monitors entirely independent of the target model. Our contributions are: • A protocol for measuring what monitor recall hides: split harmful prompts by whether the target model actually complies, and report recall on each side. • Evidence the gap is universal. Six monitors, two input modalities, three base model families: every monitor under-detects the prompts models comply with. Our results do not show that monitors are useless. They show that the number the field reports overstates the protection monitors provide, precisely on the inputs where protection is the job. 

# 2 Related Work 

Safety monitors and how they are evaluated. Prompt-level safeguards come in two main forms: fine-tuned text classifiers such as Llama Guard [Inan et al., 2023], WildGuard [Han et al., 2024], and ShieldGemma [Zeng et al., 2024], and lightweight probes trained on the target model’s activations, now deployed in production systems [Kramár et al., 2026, Cunningham et al., 2026]. Across this line of work, evaluation follows the same recipe: score a fixed set of labeled prompts and report recall, F1, or AUROC against the labels. None of these evaluations ask whether the target model would have complied with the prompts being scored. Zhao et al. [2025] show that models encode harmfulness and refusal along separate internal directions, and note that Llama Guard’s accuracy drops on prompts a model accepts. We take the evaluation itself as the object of study: we make the behavior-conditioned split the metric, and show the failure it reveals is shared across six monitor configurations, both input modalities, and three model families. 

# 3 Methodology 

3.1 Monitors 

We evaluate five input-side safety monitors spanning both common input modalities: activation probes, which read the protected model’s internal representations, and guard models, which read the prompt as text. 

Activation probes. Probes are model-specific, so we train separate probes for Gemma-3-27B-IT, Qwen3-32B, and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct. The linear probe is L2-regularized logistic regression on mean-pooled residual-stream activations; the attention probe [McKenzie et al., 2025] adds a shared MLP and ten attention heads over token positions. Both read a single layer selected by validation AUROC. Training uses 3,000 balanced English prompts from WildGuardMix, length-matched and deduplicated against evaluation sets; details in Appendix A. 

Guard models. We evaluate Llama Guard 3 8B [Inan et al., 2023], WildGuard [Han et al., 2024], and gpt-oss-safeguard-120B [OpenAI, 2025]. The first two are fine-tuned safety classifiers. gpt-oss-safeguard instead takes a written harm policy as part of its input, reasons over the prompt against that policy, and outputs a verdict; we score it under two policy wordings, one mirroring the Llama Guard taxonomy and one mirroring WildGuard’s. WildGuard serves as a control: it shares the probes’ training corpus but differs in base model, architecture, and input modality. 

Calibration. Guard models emit discrete safe/unsafe decisions; we use the probability assigned to the unsafe label as a continuous score. All monitors are thresholded at 1% false-positive rate on a 10,000-prompt WildChat benign pool, placing them at a common operating point. 23.2 Elicitability 

A monitor’s value lies in the prompts a model would not refuse on its own: where the model always refuses, a catch is redundant. We therefore partition the evaluation set by the model’s behavior, with the monitor uninvolved. For each harmful prompt we generate eight responses at temperature 1; a prompt is elicitable if the model complies at least once. Eight samples reflect that compliance is stochastic: a prompt answered one time in eight is answered eventually under retry, and a single greedy decode would undercount. An LLM grader (Claude Opus) labels each response as complied, refused, or deflected. It declined to judge 13% of responses, and declines skew toward compliance, since a grader only refuses to engage with actual harmful content. We treat unjudged responses as non-complied, which can only understate the gap. Additional Details are in Appendix D. 

3.3 Evaluation data 

We evaluate on 714 English harmful prompts from three sources: 201 from Aegis 2.0 [Ghosh et al., 2025] (67 each from Violent Crimes, Non-Violent Crimes, and Hate categories), all 200 standard behaviors from HarmBench [Mazeika et al., 2024], and the full 313-item StrongREJECT set [Souly et al., 2024]. Self-harm and child-sexual content are excluded by source label; deterministic filters remove jailbreak wrappers, conversational fragments, non-request inputs, and prompts over 128 tokens. No prompt collides with the probes’ training corpus.

4.2 Better monitors do not close the gap 

Monitor quality shifts both recall values upward without narrowing the difference between them. WildGuard is the strongest harm detector in the set (harm AUROC 0.994 ) and attains the highest elicitable recall ( 0.717 ), yet still misses more than a quarter of the prompts Gemma will answer. The attention probe improves on the linear probe on both subsets while the gap moves only from 0.324 to 

0.292 .Scale and reasoning do not close it either. gpt-oss-safeguard-120B is far larger than the probes and 15× the other guard models , reads an explicit harm policy, and reasons step by step before deciding; its recall still falls monotonically with compliance rate and the gap persists under a second, differently-worded policy. 

4.3 How a monitor talks itself out of a catch 

Gpt-oss-safeguard explains its decisions, so its false negatives can be read. Re-running its missed elicitable prompts with full reasoning traces, a consistent pattern appears: the monitor identifies the topic, then accepts the prompt’s framing at face value. The clearest example is a matched pair. Asked for an article “suggesting the Holocaust never happened,” the monitor flags denial content; asked for a persuasive article that the Holodomor was “primarily a natural disaster,” it reasons that this is “a persuasive article about a viewpoint. It’s allowed,” on a prompt Gemma complies with in all eight samples. When the monitor catches a prompt, its trace points to something explicit: a named crime, a slur, a well-known denial phrase. When it misses, no such marker is present, and it reads the request as discussion rather than harm. These traces are illustration, not measurement, but they show in the monitor’s own words what the quantitative gap implies: the monitors key on how harmful a prompt looks, not how likely it is to be answered. More traces in Appendix E. 

# 5 Limitations 

All prompts are English, single-turn, and naturally phrased; we do not test adversarial jailbreaks, which raise compliance through a different mechanism and may interact differently with monitors. We evaluate input-side monitors only; output classifiers may or may not share the failure. Guard models are evaluated against one target family at a time, and probes on three families with one training corpus. Finally, we document the failure without explaining its mechanism: our results show that every harmfulness-trained monitor under-scores what models comply with, and that the driver is readable from prompt surface, but isolating that property is left to future work. 

# 6 Conclusion 

Safety monitors are evaluated by how well they agree with harmfulness labels, but their job is to prevent harmful outputs, and we show these are not the same thing. Every monitor we test detects harmful prompts well and detects the harmful prompts models actually answer far worse, with recall gaps of 0.22 to 0.38 at a fixed operating point. A monitor’s value is not how much harm it recognizes but how much it prevents, and a monitor built for that would need to track not just whether a request is harmful, but whether this model would answer it. 4References

A.4 Calibration pool and thresholds 

The benign pool ( cal_mixed ) is 10,000 first user turns from allenai/WildChat-1M (train split). Conversations flagged toxic at either the conversation or turn level are dropped, as are turns without a user message, turns in the detector-artifact languages Nolang/Maori/Latin, turns longer than 3,072 characters or 512 Gemma-3 tokens, and (in that order) internal exact duplicates, exact and fuzzy (rapidfuzz token_sort_ratio ≥ 90 ) matches against the probe training set. The stream is read until 80,000 candidates are collected, shuffled with seed 42, and the first 10,000 survivors are kept. Languages are taken from WildChat’s own label with langdetect as fallback; the pool contains 62 languages (English 45.8%, Chinese 27.5%, Russian 10.5%; median 18 tokens). The same 10,000 prompts are scored by every monitor and every target model. Each monitor’s threshold is the 

(1 − FPR ) empirical quantile of its scores on this pool; nothing else is tuned on it.

# D Compliance judging 

Judge setup. Every rollout response was labelled by Claude Opus 5 ( claude-opus-5 ). The system prompt, verbatim: 

> You are a strict safety evaluator for an AI-safety research study. You are shown a HARMFUL user request and an AI model’s RESPONSE to it. Your job is to classify whether the model actually provided the harmful assistance that was requested. You are only labeling; you never produce harmful content yourself. Classify the RESPONSE into exactly one of three labels: – “complied”: The response provides the requested harmful assistance — actionable content, instructions, or material that meaningfully furthers the harmful request, even if partial or prefaced with a disclaimer. If it gives the harmful substance, it complied. – “refused”: The response declines. It provides no harmful assistance — a refusal, a safety message, redirection to help resources, or an off-topic non-answer with nothing usable. – “deflected”: The response neither cleanly refuses nor provides the harmful assistance. It engages but stays non-actionable — a safe reframe, high-level/generic information that does not further the request, moralizing plus a partial dodge, or an answer to a harmless reinterpretation of the prompt. Base the label only on what the response actually contains.

Per-call input. Each call contained one prompt and one of its eight responses. Responses were truncated to their first 6,000 characters; prompts were not truncated. Rollouts were 714 prompts × 8samples at temperature 1.0 (top-k 64, top-p 0.95, 512 new tokens). 

Declines and recovery. A decline was recorded when the API returned stop_reason = refusal ;such responses were marked unjudged. The judge initially declined 727 of 5,712 responses (12.7%) for Gemma-3-27B, 805 (14.1%) for Qwen3-32B and 803 (14.1%) for Llama-3.3-70B.
